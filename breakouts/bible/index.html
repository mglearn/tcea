How It's Taught

Academic, not devotional

The U.S. Supreme Court (Abington v. Schempp, 1963) has long held that public schools may teach about the Bible's literary and historical influence, so long as the approach analyzes texts rather than promotes belief. The TEA makes the same claim about its adopted materials. This suite is built entirely on that line.

What we do

Study the text

Sequence, theme, genre, archetype, allusion, rhetoric. Students ask what the story says and how it's built — the same questions they'd bring to Aesop, Homer, or Shakespeare.

What we avoid

Never devotional

No prayer, no truth claims, no "what this means for your faith." Where the text and a common interpretation diverge — as with Esther and "religious freedom" — students are taught to notice the gap, not resolve it.

Why it matters

Every student included

Framing these as literature keeps the classroom open to students of every faith and none. The 9–12 breakout makes that constitutional reasoning an explicit object of study.

The Method

CLEAR critical thinking

Every lock walks students through one move in the CLEAR cycle. By the win screen they've run the whole loop at least once — make a claim, choose a lens, cite evidence, weigh an alternative, respond.

Research-Based Design

High-effect-size strategies, built in

Each breakout is engineered around instructional moves with strong effect sizes in Hattie's Visible Learning synthesis — not bolted on, but structural to how the locks work.

d ≈ 0.75

Classroom discussion & feedback

Every lock gives immediate, specific feedback, and the "why" panel models the reasoning — turning each attempt into a feedback loop, one of the highest-leverage moves in the research.

d ≈ 0.60

Concept sorting & evidence classification

The "check all the strong evidence" locks are classification tasks: students sort relevant from irrelevant, the decoy from the real. Categorizing to find structure is a well-supported deep-learning strategy.

d ≈ 0.55

Metacognitive & strategy instruction

CLEAR names the thinking move at each step. Making the strategy visible — "this is the Alternatives step" — is what pushes students from doing the task to understanding how they did it.
