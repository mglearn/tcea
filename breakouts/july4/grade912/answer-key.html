    According to Sources A and E, where do both Locke and the Declaration say legitimate government power comes from?

⚖️ Ratification Deadlock

ratification-student.html
  1. Lock 1 — Identify the Position [mc]
    Which group demanded that a written bill of rights be added to the Constitution?
    Answer: "The Anti-Federalists" (option 2)
    Why: The sources state the Anti-Federalists' central demand was a written bill of rights to protect individual liberties against a strong central government.
  2. Lock 2 — Count the Amendments [digit]
    How many amendments make up the Bill of Rights? (Type the number.)
    Answer: 10
    Why: The Bill of Rights is the first ten amendments, ratified in 1791. The source states this directly.
  3. Lock 3 — The Series of Essays [word]
    What was the name of the essay series written to argue FOR ratification? The "______ Papers." Type one word.
    Answer: federalist
    Why: The pro-ratification essays by Madison, Hamilton, and Jay were the Federalist Papers. The missing word is Federalist.
  4. Lock 4 — Sequence the Resolution [seq]
    Put the ratification story in the correct order.
    Answer: draft → debate → promise → ratify
    Why: The sequence: Constitution drafted (1787) → public ratification debate → promise to add a bill of rights to win approval → the Bill of Rights added (1791). The compromise is what broke the deadlock.

📚 The Historian's Dilemma

historian-student.html
  1. Lock 1 — Fact vs. Interpretation [mc]
    Which of these is an ESTABLISHED FACT rather than an interpretation?
    Answer: "The Declaration was adopted on July 4, 1776" (option 3)
    Why: The adoption date (July 4, 1776) is a verifiable, settled fact. The "mainly about X" claims are interpretations historians debate, and "wisest ever" is a value judgment — neither is an established fact.
  2. Lock 2 — Name the Lens [word]
    Historian A emphasizes taxes, trade, and merchant interests. In ONE word, A's explanation is primarily ______ (type the adjective A stresses).

    Why: Historian A's thesis centers on economic grievances — taxes, trade, merchant interests. The word is economic.
  3. Lock 3 — Judge the Argument [mc]
    On what basis should you decide which historian's argument is stronger?
    Answer: "The quality, relevance, and explanatory power of the evidence" (option 2)
    Why: The weighing clue is explicit: judge by the quality and relevance of evidence and how well it explains the sources — not by patriotism, fame, or presentation.
  4. Lock 4 — Sort the Statements [multi]

    Answer: Select: #1, #2 (reject the rest)
    Why: The two "driven mainly by..." claims are interpretations historians argue over. The 1791 date is an established fact (not an interpretation), and a documentary's confidence settles nothing. Correctly, only the two theses are interpretations.

🧪 The Rhetoric Lab

rhetoric-student.html
  1. Lock 1 — Name the Appeal [mc]
    The Declaration's itemized list of specific grievances against the king is primarily which appeal?
    Answer: "Logos (logic and evidence)" (option 3)
    Why: A structured, evidence-based list of specific complaints is an appeal to LOGOS — building a case through reason and evidence, as Excerpt 1 describes.
  2. Lock 2 — The Emotional Appeal [word]
    What is the ONE-word name for an appeal to the audience's emotions? Type it.
    Answer: pathos
    Why: An appeal to emotion is pathos, as defined in the appeals clue and shown in Excerpt 2.
  3. Lock 3 — Spot the Fallacy [mc]
    Excerpt 4 says: 'If you question the plan, you must be unpatriotic.' What is wrong with this argument?
    Answer: "It attacks the person and uses bandwagon pressure instead of evidence" (option 2)
    Why: The flyer substitutes a personal attack and bandwagon pressure ("everyone patriotic agrees") for actual evidence. That is a fallacy — not a sound argument — as the clue explains.
  4. Lock 4 — Match the Techniques [multi]
    Select EVERY item that is a legitimate rhetorical APPEAL used persuasively. Reject the fallacy and the irrelevant one.
    Answer: Select: #1, #2, #3 (reject the rest)
    Why: Ethos, pathos, and logos are the three legitimate appeals, each shown in the excerpts. The "unpatriotic" line is a fallacy, and shouting louder is irrelevant to validity. Naming techniques correctly — and flagging the fallacy — is the goal.
