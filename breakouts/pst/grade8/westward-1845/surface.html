Phase 1 — Build the knowledge

Before students can reason about the expansion problem, they need the raw material: the words, the reasons Americans looked west, and the geography of the continent. These three activities are fast and front-loaded — the goal is acquisition, not yet analysis.

🎯 By the end of Phase 1 students can use the key vocabulary, explain what "Manifest Destiny" meant, name how the U.S. gained Texas, Oregon, and the Mexican Cession, and read a map/table of U.S. territorial growth to see why expansion was contested.
Vocabulary & feedback · d 0.62§113.20(c)(6)

1 · Word bank & vocabulary sort

Introduce and let students sort the unit vocabulary. Sort twice: first by "words about land & movement" vs. "words about deciding & dividing," then by student-invented categories (feedback on their reasoning).

Jigsaw method · d 0.92§113.20(c)(6), (c)(7), (c)(29)(A)

2 · Jigsaw reading — The pull west

Split the class into four expert groups, each studying one topic below, then re-mix into home groups where every topic is represented. Each expert teaches their group. (Jigsaw is one of the highest-leverage surface moves precisely because students must teach.)

A · Manifest Destiny & the idea of expansion

In the 1840s many Americans came to believe it was the nation's destiny to spread across the continent — an idea used to justify moving west, whatever the cost to others.

📄 DocsTeach · Manifest Destiny documents ↗
📄 Library of Congress · National Expansion & Reform overview ↗

B · Texas annexation & the war with Mexico

The U.S. annexed Texas in 1845, then went to war with Mexico (1846–1848). The war ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which handed the U.S. a vast cession of land.

📄 National Archives · Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ↗
📄 DocsTeach · Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo document ↗

C · The peoples already there

The West was not empty. Native nations had lived on these lands for generations, and tens of thousands of Mexican citizens lived in the territory the U.S. took — expansion moved onto their homelands.

📄 DocsTeach · Westward Expansion documents ↗
📄 National Park Service · Santa Fe Trail (Native & Mexican borderlands) ↗

D · Slavery & the territories (free vs. slave)

Every new territory reopened one question: would it allow slavery? The Wilmot Proviso tried to ban slavery in any land taken from Mexico — and split the nation along sectional lines.

📄 DocsTeach · The Wilmot Proviso document ↗
📄 Library of Congress · Pre–Civil War slavery ↗

Check for understanding: each home group writes one sentence answering "Give one reason Americans pushed west and one reason expansion divided the country."

Direct instruction · d 0.56§113.20(c)(10), (c)(11), (c)(29)(D)

3 · Map & data read — how the nation grew, 1783–1853

Give students a map of U.S. territorial growth from 1783 to 1853 plus a short data set (territory added, or population). Have them shade each acquisition and answer: which additions were the largest? which came by treaty, and which by war? Then connect geography to the debate: mountains, rivers, and distance shaped how people migrated, and each new region reopened the slavery question. Introduce free vs. slave territory as students see the land grow.
