# Turn Assessment Data Into Action
## Practice Scenarios — score these, then enter your scores in the spreadsheet

Each scenario gives you a short story or context, five questions, and five real student answers to score.
For every student, decide **1** (correct/proficient) or **0** (not yet) on each question, using the answer key note.
Then open the spreadsheet, pick the matching tab, and type your scores. Export to CSV and hand it to the chatbot for a report.

**Phases:** each question is tagged *surface* (facts, vocabulary, basic skills), *deep* (connecting and explaining), or *transfer* (applying to a new situation).

## Scenario 1 · K-2 · Reading: Story Retelling

*3 surface · 1 deep · 1 transfer*

**The context:** The class read The Little Red Hen. The Little Red Hen finds some wheat and asks her friends the cat, the dog, and the pig to help her plant it, harvest it, and bake it into bread. Each time, they say "Not I!" So she does all the work herself. When the bread is ready, everyone wants to eat it, but the Little Red Hen says no and eats it with her chicks.

**Questions and what a correct answer looks like:**

1. **(surface)** Who are the characters in the story?
   - *Correct means:* Names the Little Red Hen and at least one other animal (cat, dog, pig).
2. **(surface)** What did the Little Red Hen do first, next, and last?
   - *Correct means:* Gives events in order: plant/find wheat, then harvest, then bake.
3. **(surface)** Where does the story take place?
   - *Correct means:* Identifies a farm or the Hen's home.
4. **(deep)** Why did the Little Red Hen not share the bread?
   - *Correct means:* Explains that the friends refused to help, so they didn't earn it.
5. **(transfer)** If you were the Little Red Hen, what would you do, and why?
   - *Correct means:* Gives a choice AND a reason connected to fairness or helping.

**Sample student answers to score:**

*Student S03*

*Student S04*

   - Q5: I would share because sharing is nice.

## Scenario 6 · 6-8 · Science: Ecosystems & Energy Flow

*3 surface · 1 deep · 1 transfer*

**The context:** A middle-school science class studied food chains and energy flow in a grassland ecosystem: grass, grasshoppers, mice, hawks. This entry ticket checks understanding before a unit on human impact.

**Questions and what a correct answer looks like:**

1. **(surface)** In a food chain, what is a producer?
   - *Correct means:* Defines producer as an organism that makes its own food (e.g. grass/plants).
2. **(surface)** In grass -> grasshopper -> mouse -> hawk, which is the top consumer?
   - *Correct means:* Answers the hawk.
3. **(surface)** Put these in order from producer to top consumer: mouse, grass, hawk, grasshopper.
   - *Correct means:* Orders grass, grasshopper, mouse, hawk.
4. **(deep)** Why is there less energy available to the hawk than to the grasshopper?
   - *Correct means:* Explains energy is lost at each level, so less reaches the top.
5. **(transfer)** If a disease killed most of the mice, what would happen to the hawks and the grasshoppers? Explain.
   - *Correct means:* Predicts hawks decline (less food) and grasshoppers rise (fewer predators), with reasoning.

**Sample student answers to score:**

*Student S01*
   - Q1: A producer makes its own food like grass using the sun.
   - Q2: hawk
   - Q3: grass, grasshopper, mouse, hawk
   - Q4: Because energy is lost as heat at each step, so only a little reaches the hawk.
   - Q5: Hawks would go down because they lose their food, and grasshoppers might go up because fewer mice eat them.

*Student S02*
   - Q1: A plant.
   - Q2: hawk
   - Q3: grass, grasshopper, mouse, hawk
   - Q4: The hawk is bigger.
   - Q5: The hawks would be hungry.

*Student S03*
   - Q1: An organism that makes food through photosynthesis.
   - Q2: the hawk
   - Q3: grass, grasshopper, mouse, hawk
   - Q4: Only about 10% of energy passes to the next level, so the hawk gets the least after several transfers.
   - Q5: Hawks would decrease since mice are their main food. Grasshoppers could increase because mice ate them, but grass might get eaten more too.

*Student S04*
   - Q1: Grass.
   - Q2: hawk
   - Q3: grass, grasshopper, mouse, hawk
   - Q4: (blank)
   - Q5: Everything dies.

*Student S05*
   - Q1: Makes its own food.
   - Q2: mouse
   - Q3: grass, grasshopper, mouse, hawk
   - Q4: Because the hawk is far from the grass.
   - Q5: Hawks go down, grasshoppers go up because the mice that ate them are gone.

## Scenario 7 · 6-8 · ELA: Argumentative Writing

2. **(surface)** Name one piece of evidence the author uses.

3. **(deep)** How does that evidence support the claim?
   - *Correct means:* Connects the evidence to the claim with reasoning.
4. **(deep)** What is one counterargument someone might raise?
   - *Correct means:* Offers a plausible opposing point (e.g. buses, sports schedules).
5. **(transfer)** Write a claim and one piece of evidence for a different topic: should students have homework on weekends?

## Scenario 9 · 9-12 · Biology: Cellular Respiration

*3 surface · 1 deep · 1 transfer*

**The context:** A high-school biology class studied cellular respiration: glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain. This entry ticket checks understanding before a lab on fermentation.

**Questions and what a correct answer looks like:**

1. **(surface)** What is ATP and what is its role in the cell?
   - *Correct means:* Identifies ATP as the cell's energy currency/molecule.
2. **(surface)** What are the reactants and products of cellular respiration?
   - *Correct means:* Reactants: glucose + oxygen; products: carbon dioxide + water + ATP.
3. **(surface)** Where in the cell does the Krebs cycle occur?
   - *Correct means:* Answers the mitochondria (matrix).
4. **(deep)** Explain how glycolysis connects to the Krebs cycle.
   - *Correct means:* Explains glycolysis produces pyruvate, which feeds into the Krebs cycle.
5. **(transfer)** A cell is deprived of oxygen. Predict what happens to its ATP production and why.
   - *Correct means:* Predicts ATP drops / shifts to fermentation because the electron transport chain needs oxygen.

**Sample student answers to score:**

*Student S01*
   - Q1: ATP is the energy molecule cells use to power reactions.
   - Q2: Glucose and oxygen in; carbon dioxide, water, and ATP out.
   - Q3: The mitochondria.
   - Q4: Glycolysis breaks glucose into pyruvate, which enters the Krebs cycle in the mitochondria.
   - Q5: Without oxygen, the electron transport chain stops, so ATP drops sharply and the cell switches to fermentation for a little ATP.

*Student S02*
   - Q1: Energy.
   - Q2: Glucose and oxygen.
   - Q3: Mitochondria.
   - Q4: They're connected.
   - Q5: The cell dies.

*Student S03*
   - Q1: ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the cell's main energy currency, releasing energy when a phosphate is removed.
   - Q2: Reactants: glucose + O2. Products: CO2 + H2O + ATP.
   - Q3: Mitochondrial matrix.
   - Q4: Glycolysis in the cytoplasm splits glucose into two pyruvate molecules, which are converted to acetyl-CoA that enters the Krebs cycle.
   - Q5: ATP production falls to only what glycolysis/fermentation provides, because without O2 the ETC can't run, and NAD+ is regenerated by fermentation.

*Student S04*
   - Q1: The energy of the cell.
   - Q2: Oxygen and sugar.
   - Q3: (blank)
   - Q4: Glycolysis makes stuff for the Krebs cycle.
   - Q5: Less ATP.

*Student S05*
   - Q1: ATP stores and gives energy for cell work.
   - Q2: Glucose + oxygen -> CO2 + water + energy.
   - Q3: Mitochondria.
   - Q4: Glycolysis produces pyruvate which the Krebs cycle uses.
   - Q5: ATP goes down a lot because the last stage needs oxygen; the cell does anaerobic respiration instead.

## Scenario 10 · 9-12 · ELA: Rhetorical Analysis

*2 surface · 2 deep · 1 transfer*

**The context:** Students read a famous short speech excerpt and analyzed how the speaker persuades. This entry ticket checks whether they can identify and evaluate rhetorical choices before writing an analysis essay.

**Questions and what a correct answer looks like:**

1. **(surface)** Identify one appeal in the excerpt: ethos, pathos, or logos.
   - *Correct means:* Correctly names an appeal present in the text.
2. **(surface)** What is the tone of the excerpt?
   - *Correct means:* Names an appropriate tone (e.g. urgent, hopeful).
3. **(deep)** Analyze one rhetorical choice the speaker makes and its purpose.
   - *Correct means:* Identifies a specific device and explains its intended effect.
4. **(deep)** How effective is that choice for the speaker's audience? Explain.
   - *Correct means:* Evaluates effectiveness with reasoning tied to the audience.
5. **(transfer)** Choose a different audience (e.g. young children). How would you rewrite the appeal to fit them?
   - *Correct means:* Adapts the rhetorical strategy appropriately to a new audience.

**Sample student answers to score:**

*Student S01*

   - Q2: Urgent and determined.
   - Q3: The speaker repeats a phrase to build emotional momentum and make the idea memorable.
   - Q4: It's effective because the audience was fighting for a cause and the repetition unites them and stirs feeling.
   - Q5: For young children I'd use a simple repeated line and a picture, like 'We can help. We can share.' to keep it emotional but easy.

*Student S02*
   - Q1: Pathos.
   - Q2: Serious.
   - Q3: He uses big words.
   - Q4: It works.
   - Q5: I would make it shorter.

*Student S03*

*Student S04*

   - Q2: (blank)

   - Q4: (blank)
   - Q5: Make it for kids.

*Student S05*
   - Q1: Pathos and repetition.
   - Q2: Passionate.
   - Q3: The repetition of a key line emphasizes the main hope and makes it stick.
   - Q4: It's mostly effective because it's memorable, though some listeners might want more specifics.
   - Q5: For younger kids I'd use a short chant they can repeat so they feel included, keeping the emotional appeal.
