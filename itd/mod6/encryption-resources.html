Make encryption part of the procedure

Encryption is most useful when it is routine, not an emergency response after a device disappears. Establish clear procedures for personally identifiable information, credentials, exported student or staff data, and other sensitive records.

  • Assess current paper and digital handling practices
  • Define where sensitive data may be stored and shared
  • Encrypt sensitive files before transfer or cloud storage
  • Use a separate, secure channel to share passwords or keys
  • Develop an incident response process before an incident occurs
  • Provide recurring professional learning and practice

Texas breach-notification caution

Use encryption as a form of “safe harbor” when sensitive personal information is lost or stolen. Treat that as a reason to encrypt, not as a blanket promise that every encrypted-data incident is exempt from reporting.

Use current district policy, legal counsel, and current Texas law when deciding notification duties.

Communication

Email encryption tips

Do not put a sensitive decrypted attachment and its password in the same compromised channel.

  • Prefer district-approved secure sharing whenever available
  • Encrypt sensitive attachments before transmission
  • Exchange passwords or keys through a separate channel
  • Confirm the recipient before sending
  • Remove unnecessary downloaded copies after use

Resilience

Backups to local and cloud storage

Encryption protects confidentiality. Backups protect availability. Sensitive backup sets may need both.

A practical backup routine

  1. Keep working files in approved storage
  2. Create a separate backup on an external drive
  3. Encrypt sensitive backup sets
  4. Test restoration periodically
  5. Rotate or replace aging media rather than trusting one device forever

Backup resources

FreeFileSync can compare and synchronize folders across Windows, macOS, and Linux. The original module also links a TCEA Backups Made Simple guide and FreeFileSync tutorials.

At rest

Encrypted containers and disk encryption

Choose the smallest scope that meets the need: a file, a folder/archive, an encrypted container, or an entire disk.

File or archive encryption

Good when only selected exports or folders need protection. Tools: 7-Zip, Keka, SSE.

Encrypted container

Useful when you want a protected virtual volume for a collection of sensitive files. Tool: VeraCrypt.

Whole-disk encryption

Windows BitLocker and Apple FileVault protect an entire device. Follow district standards for managed devices and key recovery.

The original module discouraged BitLocker and FileVault based on older performance and recovery concerns. That recommendation is preserved here only as historical context, not as current guidance.

End of life

Secure deletion and data minimization

Deleting a file from a normal file browser may not make it unrecoverable. More important: avoid keeping sensitive copies you no longer need.

Why normal deletion is different

The source module links a file-recovery explainer plus recovery tools such as Recuva and Kickass Undelete to show that deleted data may be recoverable.

Tools retained from the source

File Shredder and BleachBit. For district-managed SSDs and cloud storage, use your organization’s approved deletion, retention, and device-disposal process.

Apply it

Scenarios to consider

Use these as discussion prompts. Focus on what was protected, what remains exposed, and what should happen next.

1. Lost external drive

A data clerk loses a USB drive containing an exported freshman-class dataset. The file on the drive was encrypted.

Discuss: What should be reported internally? What documentation matters? How does encryption change the risk?

2. Emailed sensitive file

A sensitive file may have been exposed through email or an account compromise.

Discuss: Was the attachment encrypted? Was the password sent separately? Who needs to be notified inside the district?

3. Printed report

An employee prints a sensitive report, carries it off-site, and stores it in a vehicle.

Discuss: What controls apply to paper records? Was the printout necessary? What is the approved disposal method?

4. Hosted data

A district relies on a third-party hosted service but has not reviewed how its data is backed up or recovered.

Discuss: Who owns backup responsibility? What does the contract say about retention, recovery, encryption, and breach response?
